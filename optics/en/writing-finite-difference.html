What is FDTD?

← All essays

While showing a simulation program I had built to a researcher at Seoul National University I work with, I heard this: “What is FDTD? So it computes with the finite-difference method. Our lab mostly uses FEM (the finite-element method), interesting.” That raised a basic question for me: what is the difference between finite differences and finite elements, and what do “difference” and “element” each mean? So I started studying. Look it up and the difference is usually summed up in one line: “finite differences approximate the equation; finite elements approximate the solution.” It is a neat slogan, but on its own it did not let me picture what actually happens inside the computer. This essay follows the finite-difference side all the way through. It starts from removing a single limit from the definition of a derivative. That small choice leads to accuracy, systems of equations and an upper limit on the time step, and finally reaches why the Yee grid of FDTD is staggered by half a cell and how the time step is actually chosen. The comparison with finite elements and finite volumes is left for the next essay.

1. What is a “difference”?

In high school, a derivative is taught like this: take x+h a little away from the point x, find the slope of the line joining the two points, then send h to 0.

f′(x)=limh→0f(x+h)−f(x)h

The only step a computer cannot do is the last one, sending h to 0. A computer does not hold a function as a whole; it only holds values at points placed apart. So the finite difference does not take the limit and stops h at a small value the size of the grid spacing. The word “finite” comes from this: h stays a finite size instead of going to 0.

The derivative symbol then disappears, and in its place only a subtraction and a division remain: “the next point's value minus this point's value, divided by the spacing.” As a formula, it is just the definition above with the limit sign removed and the equals sign changed to “approximately equal (≈).”

f′(x)≈f(x+h)−f(x)h

On a grid, each point gets a number, x₀, x₁, x₂, …, and the value at point i is written fᵢ. The same formula is then written with the values at just two neighboring numbers.

f′(xi)≈fi+1−fih

Replace every derivative in a differential equation this way, and the equation becomes an algebraic relation among the grid-point values. As the simplest example, take the way light's intensity falls at a fixed rate as it passes through an absorbing material (the Beer–Lambert law). The rate at which the intensity f falls is proportional to the current intensity, and the proportionality constant is the absorption coefficient α. Replacing the derivative on the left with the difference above and rearranging gives this.

dfdx=−αf⟶fi+1−fih=−αfi⟶fi+1=(1−αh)fi

The derivative is gone and a single multiplication remains: “the next point's intensity is the current intensity times (1 − αh).” Knowing only the entrance intensity f₀, one can compute f₁, f₂, … in turn. The true answer is the exponential e−αx, and the smaller h is made, the closer repeated multiplication by (1 − αh) gets to this exponential. This is what “approximating the equation,” mentioned at the start, means. Nowhere was it assumed that the solution is exponential; only the derivative inside the equation was touched.

But since h is not 0, this slope differs slightly from the true derivative. How much it differs, and how to shrink that difference, is the subject of the next section.

2. Which points you use decides the accuracy

There is more than one way to get a slope. Using the one point ahead, as above, is the forward difference; using the two points on both sides and taking the slope of the line joining x−h and x+h is the central difference. They involve the same number of subtractions, yet their accuracy differs greatly.

Forward difference f′(x)≈f(x+h)−f(x)hCentral difference f′(x)≈f(x+h)−f(x−h)2h

The forward difference is a slope across a width h, from x to x+h. The central difference puts x in the middle and spans x−h to x+h, a width of 2h, so its denominator is 2h.

2.1 Forward difference

Where the difference in accuracy comes from shows up in a Taylor expansion. A smooth function can be expanded as a polynomial near a point.

f(x±h)=f±hf′+h22f″±h36f‴+⋯

Here f, f′ and f″ are all values at the point x, and ± is read with the upper signs together (x+h) or the lower signs together (x−h). Put this into the forward difference. First the numerator f(x+h) − f(x): in the + expansion the leading f cancels with f(x), leaving this.

f(x+h)−f(x)=hf′+h22f″+h36f‴+⋯

Dividing by h gives the forward difference.

f(x+h)−f(x)h=f′⏟true derivative+h2f″+h26f‴+⋯⏟error

A tail is attached behind the true derivative f′. When h is small the terms from h² on are much smaller still, so the error is essentially the first term h/2·f″ and is proportional to h. When the curve bends (f″≠0), a slope that looks only ahead is pulled to one side.

2.2 Central difference

The central difference subtracts f(x−h) from f(x+h). Set the two expansions side by side: the even-order terms (f, h²f″) have the same sign and cancel in the subtraction, while the odd-order terms (hf′, h³f‴) have opposite signs and remain doubled.

f(x+h)−f(x−h)=2hf′+h33f‴+⋯

Dividing by 2h gives the central difference.

f(x+h)−f(x−h)2h=f′⏟true derivative+h26f‴+⋯⏟error

The h/2·f″ term of the forward difference has vanished entirely. The error leaning forward and the error leaning backward cancel exactly, so the remaining error is proportional to h².

The difference shows when the spacing is reduced. Making the grid twice as fine halves the error of the forward difference but cuts the error of the central difference to a quarter. Ten times finer gives a tenth and a hundredth respectively. Put the other way, for the same accuracy the central difference can space its points much farther apart. To get a slope with an error around 10⁻⁴, the forward difference must shrink h to about 10⁻⁴, while for the central difference h ≈ 10⁻² is enough. That is the difference between placing ten thousand points or a hundred on an interval of length 1. In 3D the gap grows as a cube, 10¹² points against 10⁶, a factor of a million. The side that can afford sparse points, the central difference, wins overwhelmingly in computational cost.

2.3 Second-order difference — 1, −2, 1

The second derivative can be built the same way. Read the second derivative as “the slope of the slope” and the formula follows at once. The values at three neighboring points are, from the left, f(x₀−h), f(x₀), f(x₀+h). Calling them f₋, f₀, f₊, the slope between the two right points is (f₊ − f₀)/h and the slope between the two left points is (f₀ − f₋)/h. How much the slope changed over one cell h, divided by h again, is the second derivative.

f″≈f+−f0h⏟right slope−f0−f−h⏟left slopeh=f+−2f0+f−h2

The middle point f₀ is subtracted once in the right slope and once more when the left slope is subtracted, twice in all. That is why it is −2. Each side point enters only one slope, once, so it is 1. The three coefficients add up to 0, and this is no accident either: if the three points lie on a straight line the two slopes are equal and the second derivative must be 0, and the same holds when all three values are equal. Whether it is the equation of spreading heat, the wave equation or the Helmholtz equation for light, these three numbers appear wherever a second derivative does.

Adding the two Taylor expansions from before gives the same formula. The odd orders cancel and f″ remains. Coming this way also shows that the error is proportional to h².

f″(x)≈f(x+h)−2f(x)+f(x−h)h2(error h212f(4))

3. A differential equation becomes a system of equations

Let us use it on a real problem. A current flows through a rod of length L whose two ends are dipped in ice water and held at 0 °C (Figure 3). With current flowing, Joule heat is generated equally everywhere along the rod, not at one spot. The heat per unit length q is the same along the whole rod: “uniform heating.” Heat made this way can escape only through the ice water at the two ends.

What we want here is the steady state. When the current is first switched on, the rod warms gradually, and after long enough its temperature stops changing. That does not mean heat has stopped flowing. Heat keeps being made and keeps escaping through the ends; it is just that at every point the inflow and outflow match, so only the temperature is fixed. This differs from thermal equilibrium, where the flow itself is zero. Switch the current off and all the heat leaves, bringing the whole rod to 0 °C; that is thermal equilibrium. The steady-state temperature T(x) follows the equation below: a balance in which the heat made by q escapes by conduction.

−kd2Tdx2=q,T(0)=T(L)=0

First, where this equation comes from. Heat flows from hot to cold, and the amount that flows is proportional to how steeply the temperature changes, that is, to the temperature gradient dT/dx. The proportionality constant is the thermal conductivity k. Heat runs down the gradient, so the sign is minus (Fourier's law of heat conduction).

heat flowing right=−kdTdx

Now take one thin slice of the rod from x to x+Δx (Figure 3, right). In a steady state where the temperature no longer changes, all the heat qΔx made inside the slice must escape through its two ends: “heat leaving the right end − heat entering the left end = heat made inside.”

(−kdTdx|x+Δx)−(−kdTdx|x)=qΔx

Divide both sides by Δx and the left side becomes “how much the slope changed across Δx ÷ Δx,” the slope of the slope, which is the second derivative, giving the equation at the top. In shape, the curve is hottest in the middle and cools toward the ends, bulging upward, so T″ is negative. That is why it must be multiplied by −k to match the positive heating q.

Now put this equation on a grid. Divide the rod into 6 parts of spacing h and mark 7 points (x₀…x₆). The two end points are fixed at 0, so the unknowns are the temperatures of the five inner points, T₁…T₅. At each inner point, replace the second derivative with the 1, −2, 1 formula of Section 2. Taking point 3 as an example, put x₃ in place of x in the Section 2 formula. One cell right of x₃ (x₃+h) is x₄ and one cell left (x₃−h) is x₂, so it becomes this.

d2Tdx2|x3≈T4−2T3+T2h2

Put this in place of T″ in −kT″ = q,

−kT4−2T3+T2h2=q

then multiply both sides by −h²/k and rearrange to get the equation for point 3.

T2−2T3+T4=−h2qk

The derivative has disappeared, leaving an ordinary linear equation among T₂, T₃ and T₄. The coefficients 1, −2, 1 appear for the reason seen in Section 2.3, and on the rod they carry one more, physical, meaning. By Fourier's law heat flows in proportion to the temperature difference, so the heat entering point 3 from the right neighbor is proportional to T₄ − T₃, and from the left neighbor to T₂ − T₃. Their sum, T₂ − 2T₃ + T₄, is the total heat gathering into point 3 from its neighbors. So the equation says “the heat gathering into point 3 is −h²q/k,” that is, negative: a heat balance in which point 3 sends out to its neighbors as much heat as it makes itself. This rule is the same at every point. Substituting this one “rule for exchanging heat with neighbors” (itself −2, each side 1) into points 1 through 5 in turn yields five equations. The equation for point 1 contains its left neighbor T₀, but T₀ = 0 is already fixed so it drops out, and likewise T₆ in the equation for point 5.

Point 1: −2T1+T2=−h2q/kPoint 2: T1−2T2+T3=−h2q/kPoint 3: T2−2T3+T4=−h2q/kPoint 4: T3−2T4+T5=−h2q/kPoint 5: T4−2T5=−h2q/k

Five unknowns and five equations: a system of linear equations. The right-hand side is the same −h²q/k in all five rows because the heating is uniform. Had heat been put in only at point 3, the right-hand side would appear only in row 3 and the other rows would be 0. The same unknowns are lined up in columns; erase the T's and keep only the numbers in front, and you get the matrix on the right of Figure 4. Read the matrix like this: each row is one equation (the equations for points 1–5, from the top), and each column is one unknown (T₁–T₅, from the left). The number in a cell is the coefficient multiplying that unknown in that equation. In the third row, the T₂ cell has 1, the T₃ cell −2 and the T₄ cell 1, while the T₁ and T₅ cells, which do not appear in the equation for point 3, are 0.

What stands out in this matrix is that most of it is 0, because each point's equation contains only itself and its immediate neighbors. The same holds in 3D: a box divided into 100 cells per side has a million unknowns, but each row has only 7 nonzeros, itself plus its neighbors above and below, front and back, left and right. Storing a million-by-million matrix whole would overwhelm memory, but storing only the nonzero cells lets a laptop solve it. The claim that finite differences are simple and fast comes from this structure in which only neighbors are tied together.

One bonus. The true answer to this problem is a parabola (a quadratic). The error term in Section 2 was proportional to the fourth derivative, and a quadratic has zero fourth derivative, so there is no error at all. That is why, with only 7 points, the grid-point temperatures of this rod problem match the exact values to the last decimal.

4. Dividing time into cells too sets an upper limit

The rod problem was a steady state in which time does not flow. To compute something that moves, like light, time must be divided into cells too. Consider the 1D wave equation, which describes how a vibration spreads when a string is plucked.

∂2u∂t2=c2∂2u∂x2

The spatial second derivative becomes 1, −2, 1 as before, and the temporal second derivative likewise becomes “next time − 2 × now + previous time.” Rearranging gives a formula that computes the value at the next time directly from the values now and at the previous time. The superscript n is the time cell and the subscript i is the space cell.

uin+1=2uin−uin−1+S2(ui+1n−2uin+ui−1n),S=cΔtΔx

This time there is not even a system of equations to solve. Everything on the right is already known, so substituting cell by cell gives the next time, and repeating that lets time flow. This approach is called an explicit method.

But there is a trap here. When the one knob left in the formula, S, exceeds 1, the calculation blows up. S is “the distance light travels in one time cell (cΔt)” divided by “one space cell (Δx).” Look at the formula again: the next value of cell i is decided by looking only at the three cells i−1, i, i+1, so inside the calculation information can spread at most one space cell per time cell. Yet the real wave travels cΔt in that time. If cΔt is larger than Δx, the true answer is affected by distant cells the calculation has not yet seen, and the calculation has no way of knowing. This condition, found in 1928 by Courant, Friedrichs and Lewy, is called the CFL condition after the initials of their names.

But why does the calculation's “not seeing” distant cells show up as numbers blowing up? Let us think about it with a guitar string.

First, let us draw the guitar string inside the computer. A real guitar string is smoothly continuous, but the computer remembers only the heights of a few points on it. Picture points marked on the string at spacing Δx, with each point's height written as + or − (Figure 5 (a)). Plucked, the whole string may swing as one big arc (a low note) or form nodes and swing as several waves (a high note). As in Figure 5 (b), the shorter the wave, the higher the note and the faster it vibrates. The shortest wave a string divided into points can make is a zigzag in which the points alternate up, down, up, down. There is no room to fit a wave more tightly between the points, so this is the highest note such a string can make and its fastest-vibrating shape. The computer cannot watch this vibration continuously; it sees it only as photos taken one every Δt (Figure 5 (c)). S = cΔt/Δx expresses how far apart those photos are.

It is enough to test the zigzag alone. Any shape is a superposition of waves of various wavelengths, and among them the fastest-vibrating zigzag is the first to fail. The dense pattern that fills the screen when the calculation blows up in Figure 7 later is also this zigzag.

Read the formula in two parts. Rewrite the update formula from before, moving only the right and left terms to the front inside the parentheses, and it splits into two pieces.

uin+1=2uin−uin−1⏟Inertia+S2(ui+1n+ui−1n−2uin)⏟Pull

The superscript n is the photo number and the subscript i is the point number. In words, it reads like this: the next height of a point is the sum of “inertia” and “pull.”

next=2×now−previous⏟Inertia+S2×(right+left−2×now)⏟Pull
  • Inertia 2×now − previous: where the point would be if it moved one more photo as far as it moved from the previous photo to the current one. It is the same as extending the line through the two points by one cell.
  • Pull S² × (left + right − 2×now): how much it is pulled toward its neighbors. If it is higher than its neighbors it is pulled down; if lower, up. The longer the interval between photos, the longer it is pulled between one photo and the next, so the pull is multiplied by S².

The formula needs two photos, now and previous, to compute the next, so the first two must be given to start. Here we start from a zigzag that flips up and down every photo: the middle point is at −1 previously and +1 now, and its neighbors are always the opposite. From then on, the formula computes everything.

The basic case, S = 1. Inertia is 2×(+1) − (−1) = +3, and since both neighbors are −1 the pull is 1 × (−1 − 1 − 2) = −4. The next height is 3 − 4 = −1. The pull brings the inertia back to exactly the same height on the opposite side, so +1, −1, +1, −1 repeats forever and the size stays at 1.

When S is less than 1, say S = 0.9. Inertia is still +3, but the pull weakens to −4 × 0.81 = −3.24, so the next height is −0.24. Pulled too little, it does not reach the other side. Continuing gives −0.70, +1.11, −0.68, −0.27, +1.01: what used to flip every photo now swings back and forth slowly over several photos, but the size stays around 1. Having gone less far, its momentum into the next photo is that much smaller, so the shortfall does not accumulate but returns each time. The calculation is safe; what goes wrong is that a short wave that should vibrate fast vibrates slowly. This is also why ripples trail behind the pulse when S is set to 0.5 in Figure 7.

When S is greater than 1, say S = 1.05. Inertia is again +3, but the pull strengthens to −4 × 1.1025 = −4.41, so the next height is −1.41. It cannot stop at −1 on the other side and goes past. The downward momentum has grown by the overshoot, and the neighbors have moved farther to the opposite side, so the pull has grown too. So at the next photo it overshoots again. +2.40, −4.37, +8.13: at each flip, the overshoot becomes the stake for the next overshoot and the size balloons.

In all three cases the inertia is the same +3; only whether the pull is weaker or stronger than −4 differs. Even with a different starting shape, say holding the zigzag by hand and letting go (previous = now = +1), only the numbers change; the conclusion that S < 1 stays within a fixed size while S > 1 balloons is the same. And because the zigzag's neighbors are always on the opposite side, the pull acts on it more strongly than on any other shape. So when S exceeds 1, this shape is the first to overshoot.

Now check it in an actual calculation. Figure 7 places a single pulse in the middle and repeats the update formula of Section 4 as is. The pulse contains only a tiny amount of zigzag at first. So with S set slightly above 1, it looks fine for a while, until at some moment the zigzag outgrows the pulse and bursts through the screen.

So far everything is 1D. How the limit on S changes in 2D and 3D, and what S is actually set to within the range where nothing blows up, is covered in Section 6, after FDTD.

5. Stagger by half a cell and you get FDTD

Now light. Maxwell's equations are not second derivatives but two first derivatives meshed together. For light traveling in the x direction, keeping only the electric field E and the magnetic field H leaves the following two equations.

∂H∂t=1μ∂E∂x,∂E∂t=1ε∂H∂x

The time change of E is set by the spatial slope of H, and the time change of H by the spatial slope of E. These first derivatives must be turned into differences, and as seen in Section 2, accuracy calls for central differences. But if E and H sit at the same points, the central-difference slope of E must use the points 2h apart on both sides, and the middle point itself goes unused. That throws away half the information.

The solution Kane Yee published in 1966 was to place E and H staggered by half a cell in both space and time. E sits at integer cells (i) and integer times (n), H at half cells (i+½) and half times (n+½). Then the slope of E that H needs is the difference between the two E points on either side of H, a central difference of spacing h. Time works the same way: the E needed to advance H from n−½ to n+½ sits exactly at the middle time n. It is an arrangement in which every derivative automatically becomes a central difference.

Eliminate H from these two update formulas and, remarkably, you get a formula identical, letter for letter, to the wave formula of Section 4. What ran in Figure 7 was in fact 1D FDTD. The pulse moving perfectly at S = 1 and blowing up beyond 1 both happen in FDTD just the same.

The 3D Yee grid extends this idea to a box. Put the three components of E at the middles of the edges of a cubic cell and the three components of H at the centers of its faces, and each E component is surrounded by four H's and each H component by four E's. The grid's shape itself embodies the fact that the curl in Maxwell's equations is “the amount that circulates around.”

Using it in practice teaches one thing. Square cells cut round shapes into staircases. The curved surface of a ring resonator becomes a jagged staircase on the grid and slightly shifts the resonance wavelength. That is why programs such as MEEP average the material in boundary cells. Dividing the shape from the start into elements that follow the curve is the finite-element approach.

6. Choosing S — stability and dispersion

Section 4 looked only at a 1D string. Real FDTD is 2D or 3D. Deciding what S to use then means weighing two things separately: whether the calculation blows up (stability), and, even if it does not, how accurate it is (dispersion). Here S is cΔt/Δx when the cell size is the same Δx in every direction.

6.1 Stability — more dimensions, a lower limit

The zigzag argument of Section 4 extends directly. First, write the 1D pull in symbols. In a zigzag, if the middle point is +1 its neighbors are −1, so the bracket is always −4.

Pull=S2×(left+right−2×now)=S2×(−1−1−2)=−4S2

The −4 (S = 1), −3.24 (S = 0.9) and −4.41 (S = 1.05) computed in Section 4 are all this −4S² with S plugged in.

In 2D a point looks at its neighbors above and below as well as left and right. The shortest wave is a checkerboard pattern that alternates up and down from cell to cell, so the neighbors above and below are also −1, and one bracket is added per direction.

Pull=S2×[(left+right−2×now)⏟−4+(up+down−2×now)⏟−4]=−8S2

In 3D one more bracket, front and back, is added, giving −12S². Inertia is +3 regardless of dimension. As seen in Section 4, overshooting begins once the pull becomes stronger than −4, so the stability condition is that −4S², −8S² and −12S² respectively do not become stronger than −4.

DimensionPull on the zigzagStability condition
1D−4S²S ≤ 1
2D−8S²S ≤ 1/√2 ≈ 0.707
3D−12S²S ≤ 1/√3 ≈ 0.577

The more neighbors there are, the stronger the combined pull, so the photos must be taken closer together. This is why making the grid finer in 3D FDTD makes the calculation take much longer than expected. Halving the space cell multiplies the number of points by 8. Δt must also be halved, doubling the number of steps, for a factor of 16 in all.

6.2 Dispersion — even without blowing up, each wave has its own speed

In vacuum, light of every wavelength travels at c. On a grid, however, the shorter the wave, the slower it goes. The zigzag at S = 0.9 in Section 4 failing to reach its proper speed is the extreme case. This dependence of speed on wavelength is called numerical dispersion. Its size is set by how many points fit in one wavelength and by S. The table below gives how many percent slower a wave on the grid is than in reality.

Points per wavelength1D S = 0.51D S = 0.993D S = 0.5 along an axis3D S = 0.5 diagonal3D S = 0.57 along an axis

300.14 %0.004 %0.14 %0.015 %0.12 %

There are three things to read in the table. First, doubling the number of points cuts the error to a quarter. The h² error of the central difference from Section 2 shows through directly. Second, in 3D the error cannot be erased with S. In 1D, pushing S to 1 makes the error almost 0, but along an axis in 3D, 1.1 % remains at 10 points per wavelength even just below the limit (0.577). The knob that reduces error is, in the end, the number of points. Conversely, lowering S does not make it more accurate either. Along an axis in 3D, lowering S from 0.5 to 0.3 actually raises the error at 10 points from 1.24 % to 1.50 %. The error made by the space cell and the error made by the time cell partly cancel each other, and shrinking only the time cell breaks that cancellation. Third, in 3D the speed depends on direction. Light traveling along a grid axis is slowest and light traveling diagonally is fastest (Figure 9, right). It is a directionality that real space does not have, made by the square grid, so it is called numerical anisotropy.

The numbers in the table above are obtained by splitting the shape on the grid into waves of various wavelengths and computing, for each wave, how much it grows and how much it rotates per step. This is called von Neumann analysis. The splitting into waves uses Fourier series. The growth gives the stability condition of 6.1, and the rotation gives the dispersion in this table. Building the differences needs only the Taylor expansion of Section 2, so Fourier is needed only when you want to weigh accuracy wavelength by wavelength like this.

1 % may look small, but it grows as distance accumulates. In structures where light circles hundreds of wavelengths and phase accumulates, such as resonators, the resonance wavelength shifts by roughly that ratio. At 1550 nm, 0.3 % is 4–5 nm, and the resonance width of a ring resonator is usually much narrower than that.

6.3 So how to choose

Set accuracy with the cell size, and use S to save time. Cells are based on the wavelength inside the material, with 10–20 points per wavelength as the default. Light at the telecom wavelength of 1550 nm shrinks to about 445 nm inside silicon (refractive index about 3.48), so cells come out at 20–40 nm. For problems where phase accumulates, such as resonators, make them finer. Then shrink the cells one more step, run again, and if the result barely changes, judge that grid sufficient (a convergence test). The numbers in the table above are only a starting point; whether it is actually enough is checked this way.

S has almost no effect on accuracy, so make it as large as possible without blowing up, since the number of steps needed to simulate the same time is proportional to 1/S. But it is not set right at the limit. Adding dispersive material models or absorbing layers at the boundary (PML) can push the real limit slightly below the theoretical value, so the usual margin is 90–99 % of the limit. MEEP's default of S = 0.5 is about 87 % of the 3D limit 0.577, a generous margin.
