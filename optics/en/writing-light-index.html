How does light change a material's refractive index?

← All essays

The optical bistability essay said that whatever changes the refractive index, all the cavity sees is “which way the resonance shifts, by how much, and how fast.” This essay looks behind those numbers at what actually happens inside the material: the light’s electric field distorting the electron cloud, absorbed light heating the lattice, photons creating charges, absorbing slots filling up, and light pushing a mirror. I draw the five one by one and then put them side by side in a single table.

1. What it means for the refractive index to change

The refractive index is a number that says how much more slowly light travels in a material than in vacuum. But if you follow why light slows down, the index is really set by how strongly the charges in the material follow the light’s electric field. As light passes an atom, its field tugs the electron cloud bound to the nucleus back and forth, and the shaking electron re-emits light like a tiny antenna. This re-emitted light is slightly behind the original in phase, so when the two add up the result is a wave whose crests lag a little behind the original. Each atom adds a little more of this delay, so the wave in the material appears to travel more slowly than in vacuum. The refractive index is exactly this slowing.

For the material as a whole this shaking is the polarization P, and in weak light the polarization is exactly proportional to the electric field (P=ε0χE). If the proportionality constant χ is fixed, the refractive index n=1+χ is fixed too. That is why ordinary optics treats the refractive index as a material constant.

When light gets strong, this “fixed” breaks in two ways. One is that the electron’s motion itself stops being proportional to the field. Pull the electron cloud too hard and the response bends, like a spring that is overstretched, and the refractive index starts to follow the light’s intensity. This is called the Kerr effect. The Kerr effect happens only at the moment the light passes. The other is that the light changes the material itself. If absorbed light heats the material, creates new charges, fills the slots that can absorb, or pushes the cavity into a new shape, the changed material has a different refractive index or absorption. Here the change made by the light builds up in the material and drains away slowly, so it lingers for a while after the light is off.

I fix four yardsticks for comparing the five. Sign is whether the index goes up or down, or whether absorption changes rather than the index; this decides which way in wavelength the resonance shifts. Speed is the time for the change to appear and disappear when the light is switched on and off. Size is how much the index changes for the same light, and cost is what you give up to get that change: absorption loss, heating or noise. To say it up front, putting the five side by side shows clearly that size and speed are traded against each other.

2. The Kerr effect — the electron cloud distorts

Think of the electron cloud as a weight on the end of a spring attached to the nucleus. Pull a little and it stretches in proportion (Hooke’s law); then the polarization is proportional to the field and the index is constant. Pull hard and the spring no longer stretches exactly in proportion, because the potential the atom makes is a parabola only near the bottom and changes shape farther out. Terms proportional to the square and the cube of the field then join the polarization (P=ε0(χ(1)E+χ(2)E2+χ(3)E3)). But in materials like silicon or glass, whose atomic arrangement looks the same when inverted (they have inversion symmetry), the square term vanishes. The electron must be pushed the same way whether the field points up or down, and a square term keeps its sign when the field reverses, so it would break that symmetry. The first nonlinearity left in such materials is therefore the cube term, and the Kerr effect is exactly the effect of this cube term. Putting this term into the refractive index makes the index change in proportion to the light intensity I. Since intensity is proportional to the square of the field amplitude (I∝E2), the index change produced by the cube term is proportional to intensity.

n=n0+n2I

Its speed is the fastest of the five. The light electron cloud follows within a single optical cycle, so the index tracks the light intensity on a femtosecond scale. In a cavity, it is actually the cavity that responds more slowly, and the time light stays in the ring (tens of ps) sets the speed.

But its size is tiny. Silicon’s n2 is about 4.5×10⁻¹⁸ m²/W at the telecom wavelength (1.55 µm), and silica, the material of optical fiber (SiO₂, a glass — a different material from the semiconductor silicon), is about 2.6×10⁻²⁰ m²/W, roughly 170 times smaller still. Even squeezing 1 W into a 1 µm² cross-section to reach 10¹² W/m² changes silicon’s index by only 4.5×10⁻⁶, in the sixth decimal place. That is why Kerr devices either trap light in a cavity to build up intensity or, like optical fiber, run it over kilometers to accumulate the tiny change with length. Why the Kerr effect is so small is shown at the start of section 8 by comparing with the atom’s own field.

Its cost is the light needed to reach such intensity. On top of that, in silicon such strong light also causes the two-photon absorption of section 4, so it is hard to get a clean Kerr effect alone. Whether two-photon absorption happens is decided by whether 1.60 eV, the energy of two photons together, exceeds the material’s band gap (Figure 4). Silicon’s band gap of 1.12 eV is exceeded, while silicon nitride (about 5 eV) and silica (8 eV or more) are far out of reach. So when a device should run on the Kerr effect alone, wide-gap silicon nitride or silica is used. A wide band gap, however, also means electrons are bound more tightly to their atoms, so n₂ shrinks too and the limit of small size remains. One calculation puts a Kerr-only bistable memory in a silicon nitride ring at about 1.8 W (Yoshiki and Tanabe 2014).

Then why not use a material that polarizes more easily in the first place? There are two paths. One is to revive not the Kerr effect (the cube term) but the square term that vanished above. In a crystal where two different atoms alternate, like gallium and arsenic, the two atoms differ in electronegativity, so the bonding electrons lean to one side, and the arrangement looks different when inverted: inversion symmetry is broken. So crystals like GaAs or lithium niobate (LiNbO₃) keep a square term. The square term, however, is not used directly to change a beam’s index with that same beam’s intensity. Instead it is used for the Pockels effect, where an externally applied voltage changes the index (lithium niobate modulators), and for second-harmonic generation, which doubles the light’s frequency.

The other is to choose a material with a large Kerr effect itself, that is, a large cube term. n₂ tends to grow as the band gap narrows, that is, as electrons are bound less tightly. But if the gap is too narrow, two-photon absorption follows. That is why AlGaAs, in which aluminum is mixed into GaAs to lift the gap just above the two-photon energy (1.60 eV), is studied as a platform for a large Kerr effect without two-photon absorption (Pu et al. 2016). Either path only enlarges the effect; neither overturns the huge gap to the atomic field shown at the start of section 8.

3. Thermo-optics — absorbed light heats

Even a waveguide that looks transparent absorbs a tiny amount of light. Rough spots on the surface, defects, and the free charges of section 4 eat light, and the energy of the eaten light ends up as vibrations of the atoms, that is, heat. When the temperature rises, the refractive index changes, because the lattice expands and the electrons’ energy levels shift slightly. The coefficient that bundles these together is the thermo-optic coefficient dn/dT. For silicon at room temperature and 1.55 µm it is about 1.8×10⁻⁴ /K, positive. Heating raises the index, so the resonance shifts to longer wavelength, the same way as the Kerr effect.

How large this number is becomes clear next to the Kerr effect. A rise of just 1 K changes the index by 1.8×10⁻⁴; to get the same change from the Kerr effect would need an intensity of 4×10¹³ W/m², that is, 40 W in a 1 µm² cross-section. So when light enters a silicon microring, the Kerr effect is buried under heat and hardly visible. The first silicon-chip bistability by Almeida and Lipson in 2004, seen in section 7 of the bistability essay, was thermal too.

So how much does a ring heat up for the same light? That is decided by the path the heat takes to escape. Once the ring reaches a steady temperature, the heat coming in equals the heat going out, so the temperature rise is the absorbed light power times the thermal resistance (how hard it is for heat to escape). In a structure where heat escapes easily the ring warms only a little; in a structure that traps heat the same light warms it much more. Figure 5 puts these two cases side by side and switches the same light on and off.

Trapping heat lowers the threshold. To make bistability the resonance must be pushed by several linewidths, and for that the ring must reach a certain temperature. A ring that traps heat gets there with less light. In 2010 Sun and Reano did exactly what the right panel of Figure 5 shows, etching away the oxide under the ring to separate it from the substrate with air, and obtained hysteresis at 80 µW. A ring of the same shape that was not separated showed no bistability below 2 mW under the same conditions.

But it gets slower. Heat is large but inherently slow. To change the index the ring must actually warm up, and to return it the heat must leave, and heat spreads very slowly compared with light or electrons. So the thermal response of a silicon microring is on the microsecond scale, and Almeida and Lipson’s device was limited to a modulation bandwidth of 500 kHz. Trapping heat also makes cooling take longer, so lowering the threshold makes it slower still. Here too size and speed are traded.

Beyond slowness, there are two more costs. A small change in ambient temperature shifts the resonance in the same way, so the temperature must be held steady to use the device. And heat from one device spreads to its neighbors and they interfere. Silicon photonics also turns this property around: attaching a small electric heater beside a ring to warm it on purpose and tune the resonance to the desired wavelength is the most common way to tune silicon rings.

4. Carrier dispersion — light creates charges

For a semiconductor to absorb light, one photon’s energy must exceed the band gap. The photon lifts an electron from the valence band into the conduction band, and a hole is left where the electron was. Silicon’s band gap is 1.12 eV and a telecom photon at 1.55 µm carries 0.80 eV, so a single photon cannot lift an electron to the conduction band. That is why silicon waveguides are transparent at telecom wavelengths. Everything in this section happens inside an on-chip silicon (Si) waveguide. Silica (SiO₂), the material of optical fiber, has a band gap of 8 eV or more (Figure 4), so this hardly happens there.

But when light is very strong, two photons can be absorbed at once. Together the two carry 1.60 eV, which exceeds the band gap, and one electron and one hole are created. This absorption happens only when two photons are in the same place at the same time. Here the intensity of light is the number of photons crossing a unit area per second times the energy of one photon. One photon’s energy is set by the wavelength (0.80 eV at 1.55 µm), so at the same wavelength twice the intensity means twice as many photons. Seen as a wave, the field amplitude has grown by √2 (intensity ∝ amplitude²); seen as particles, the photons have doubled. With twice the photons, the chance of two photons meeting in the same place is four times larger, so this absorption is proportional to the square of the intensity. It is therefore almost absent in weak light and grows sharply in strong light. Silicon’s two-photon absorption coefficient at 1.55 µm is about 0.8 cm/GW.

The leftover energy becomes heat. Of the 1.60 eV from the two photons, 1.12 eV goes into crossing the band gap, and the remaining 0.48 eV lifts the electron above the bottom of the conduction band, that is, it becomes the electron’s kinetic energy. Such a hot electron collides with the lattice many times within picoseconds, hands this energy over as lattice vibrations, that is, heat, and settles to the bottom of the conduction band (in Figure 6 the electron appears high up and sinks down). So two-photon absorption makes not only charges but also the heat of section 3.

The created charges change the refractive index. Free electrons and holes, not bound to atoms, oscillate in the opposite phase to bound electrons in the light’s field, so they reduce the polarization of the material and lower the index. A bound electron has the spring of the nucleus pulling it and is pushed opposite to the field, but a free electron has no restoring spring and swings on inertia alone, moving in the same direction as the field (Figure 7). The sign is opposite to the Kerr effect and heat, so the resonance shifts to shorter wavelength. At the same time free charges also eat light directly (free-carrier absorption), so absorption rises. According to the formula Soref and Bennett (1987) fitted to silicon measurements, creating 10¹⁸ cm⁻³ each of electrons and holes at 1.55 µm lowers the index by about 3×10⁻³ and raises the absorption by about 15 cm⁻¹. The absorption coefficient α has units of cm⁻¹ because it is defined so that intensity decays as I=I0e−αz over a distance z. An α of 15 cm⁻¹ means the intensity falls by a factor of e⁻¹⁵ over 1 cm. To convert to the dB/cm used for devices, multiply by 4.34 (= 10/ln 10): about 15 cm⁻¹ is about 63 dB/cm, enough to lose roughly three quarters of the intensity in just 1 mm. As an index change it is comparable to heating by a dozen or more kelvin, and several orders of magnitude larger than the Kerr effect.

Δn = −(8.8×10⁻²² ΔN + 8.5×10⁻¹⁸ ΔP^0.8) Δα = 8.5×10⁻¹⁸ ΔN + 6.0×10⁻¹⁸ ΔP [cm⁻¹] ΔN, ΔP: electron and hole densities [cm⁻³] · silicon, 1.55 µm (Soref and Bennett 1987)

Why does having a spring or not reverse the direction? Think of a swing. Push it much more slowly than its own rhythm (natural frequency) and it follows your push, moving the way you push. Shake it back and forth much faster than its rhythm and it is too heavy to keep up, moving in exactly the opposite rhythm to your push. A bound electron has the natural frequency of its spring out in the ultraviolet, so telecom light, much slower, is the “pushing slowly” case. The electron follows the force it receives, and since it is negative the force points opposite to the field, so it moves opposite to the field. A free electron has no spring, so its natural frequency is zero, and any light is the “pushing too fast” case: it moves opposite to the force, that is, along the field. In formula form the displacement is x∝−E/(ω02−ω2), and the denominator changes sign between the bound electron (ω₀ > ω) and the free electron (ω₀ = 0).

Speed is set by the charge lifetime. Charges change the index the moment they appear, but after the light is off the electrons and holes remain until they meet again and disappear. In an ordinary silicon rib waveguide this lifetime is tens of ns. That is about a thousand times faster than heat, but the speed of Xu and Lipson’s silicon ring logic devices, seen in the bistability essay, was tied to this lifetime. The way to shorten it is to pull the charges out instead of waiting. Make p-type and n-type regions on either side of the waveguide and apply a reverse bias (a p-i-n diode), and that field sweeps electrons and holes to the two electrodes. In 2005 Rong et al. used this in a silicon Raman laser to cut the effective lifetime to about 1 ns at 25 V reverse bias. What that laser was after was loss rather than speed: charges created by two-photon absorption were eating the light and the laser would not turn on, and pulling the charges out solved it.

In short, its strength is a place between size and speed. It gets the same index change with far less light than the Kerr effect, it is about a thousand times faster than heat, and its opposite sign keeps it distinguishable from heat even when both are present.

Its cost is absorption, and not even a voltage can erase it. The reason is that the same charges both lower the index and eat the light. As the formula above shows, Δn and Δα both grow and shrink together with the charge density. Pulling the charges out with a p-i-n shortens the lifetime and reduces absorption, but the index change shrinks by as much as the accumulated charge does. So the reverse bias is not a device that removes absorption; it is a knob that reduces index change and absorption together to buy speed. It worked in Rong et al.’s Raman laser because that laser never needed the index change the charges make. A device that wants to change the index with charges, on the other hand, always has to pay for the light those charges eat, and the two-photon absorption that made the charges is itself a process that loses light.

Run the same principle electrically and you get a familiar device. A silicon modulator changes the index by injecting or removing charges with a voltage. Instead of light creating the charges, a circuit supplies them, but the physics of the index change and the absorption that comes with it are the same.

Not every material needs two photons like silicon. InGaAsP grown on InP can have its composition tuned to put the band gap near telecom wavelengths, so close to the signal wavelength it can absorb a single photon and create charges. The NTT nanocavity optical RAM (Nozaki et al. 2012) seen in the bistability essay buries a tiny piece of InGaAsP in an InP photonic crystal. The carrier-plasma index change of the charges made this way creates two stable states, used as 0 and 1. Confining charges and light together in a small space gave a high charge density from little light, which made the low power of about 30 nW possible.

5. Saturable absorption and band filling — the absorbing slots fill up

The previous three were about changing the index; this one is about changing the absorption. Absorbing light takes two things: an electron in the lower level, and an empty slot in the upper level for it to move into. In weak light the upper level is almost empty, so light is absorbed as fast as it arrives. In strong light, electrons go up faster than they come down, the upper level starts to fill, and the filled slots can no longer absorb. This shortage of absorbing slots, letting light pass straight through, is called saturable absorption. Figure 8 draws the process with the Bohr orbits of a single atom.

If the time the raised electron stays in the upper level is τ and the cross-section with which one atom catches a photon is σ, the intensity at which absorption falls to half (the saturation intensity) is Isat=hν/(στ), and the absorption coefficient falls with intensity like this.

α=α01+I/Isat

The shape of the formula tells the story. A material whose electrons stay longer (larger τ) fills its slots more easily and has a lower saturation intensity, but its slots also take that much longer to empty after the light is off. Here too, a low threshold and speed are traded through the same single τ.

In semiconductors this is called band filling. When light lifts electrons to the bottom of the conduction band, the lowest states fill first. Electrons cannot share the same state (the Pauli exclusion principle), so absorption at wavelengths just above the band gap falls. Near the wavelengths where absorption falls, the refractive index changes too. Absorption and index are not independent: when the absorption at one wavelength changes, the index at nearby wavelengths must change with it (the Kramers–Kronig relations).

That is why the history is a little tangled. The idea of putting a saturable absorber in a cavity to make bistability from absorption itself was first proposed by Szöke et al. in 1969. Yet the bistability actually observed by Gibbs et al. in sodium vapor in 1976 and in a GaAs etalon in 1979 was mainly on the refractive side. The index change from saturating a transition near resonance pushed the cavity resonance, and in GaAs band filling was the main cause. The same physics of “slots filling up” can make both absorptive and dispersive bistability. Which one you get depends on how far from the resonance you put the laser.

In optical AI it is a candidate activation function. A saturable absorber absorbs weak light strongly and passes strong light easily, so its transmission rises with intensity in a bent curve, a shape that resembles a neural network activation function. In 2017 Shen et al., proposing an MZI optical neural network, showed simulations with saturable absorbers as the nonlinearity between layers. In lasers it is already an old component: placed inside a laser cavity it lets only strong pulses survive, which is used to make short pulses (mode locking). Its cost is, as the name says, absorption. The weaker the light the more it eats, so loss is large where the signal is weak, and the eaten light becomes heat.

6. Optomechanics — light pushes the cavity

The last route changes not a property of the material but its shape. A photon has no mass but carries momentum h/λ, so when it bounces off a mirror it leaves twice that momentum in the mirror. Multiply by the number of photons hitting per second and you get a force, called radiation pressure. The force on a mirror fully reflecting light of power P is F=2P/c. Reflecting 1 W gives only about 6.7 nN, far too small to feel by hand.

A cavity amplifies this small force. Light makes thousands of round trips inside a cavity, so the stored intensity is thousands of times the incoming light, and the mirror is pushed by all of that stored light. If one mirror hangs on a spring, it retreats by the push and the cavity gets longer. A longer cavity shifts its resonance to longer wavelength. The index stays the same but the length changes and the resonance moves, in the same direction as the Kerr effect and heat. So the feedback of the bistability essay closes just the same. In 1983 Dorsel et al. observed bistability caused by radiation pressure alone in a Fabry-Perot cavity with one movable mirror.

Speed is the time it takes the mechanics to move. A mirror or ring wall must actually move, so the response time is set by the structure’s mechanical oscillation period, and smaller structures are faster. On a chip, a thin waveguide or the ring itself moves instead of a mirror. In 2008 Li et al. suspended a silicon waveguide in air and showed it bending under the force of the light passing through it (a gradient force, with light leaking out the side of the waveguide pulling toward the substrate). Its cost is noise and ringing. The lighter and more mobile the structure, the larger its random thermal jitter (Brownian motion), and a structure once pushed does not stop at once but oscillates. The field turns this around into a major line of research on cooling or measuring mechanical vibration with light (Aspelmeyer et al. 2014), but as a switch for computing it is the least used of the five.

7. A side route — turning light into electricity and back

So far all five waited for the material to respond to light by itself. There is an entirely different route: receive the light in a detector and turn it into a current, use that current to charge a capacitor into a voltage, and use that voltage to change a modulator’s absorption or index. This light → electricity → light (O-E-O) loop is called optoelectronic hybrid. The physics inside the modulator is the charges of section 4 or the absorption change of quantum wells (the quantum-confined Stark effect), but what drives it is a circuit, not a change made directly by the light.

This sidesteps the weakness of material nonlinearity. The output light is made by the separately supplied power beam, and the input light only opens and closes its gate. So the gain that lets a weak input move a strong output comes from the circuit. It is the same structure as an electronic transistor, where a small gate voltage opens and closes a large drain current flowing in from the supply; here the weak input light stands in for the gate and the power beam for the drain current. What the Miller commentary demanded of optical logic devices was, in the end, exactly these properties of an “optical transistor.” Input and output are different beams, so they do not mix, and depending on how the circuit is wired the output can follow or oppose the input. S-SEED (Lentine, Miller et al. 1989) is the classic of this approach: two quantum-well p-i-n diodes in series, with the state set by the ratio of two beams. That structure is exactly why S-SEED passed the Miller criteria that cavity bistability missed, as section 8 of the bistability essay discusses.

Its cost is the energy to charge the capacitor, roughly CV2. So the task of this route is how small the combined capacitance of detector, wiring and modulator can be made. In 2019 Nozaki et al. at NTT attached detectors and modulators on a photonic crystal and cut the total capacitance to about 2 fF. With O-E-O transistors that have optical gain, they demonstrated an optical switch, a wavelength converter and a cascadable optical repeater at energies on the order of femtojoules per bit. As a quick calculation, 1 V across 2 fF gives C·V² = 2 fJ. Because this route gets its nonlinearity from a circuit, strictly it does not stand in the same row as the five above. But it competes for the same goal of “changing light with light,” so it is included here.

8. All in one table

Before the table, let me address why changing light with light is so hard, and in particular why the Kerr effect is so small. Light passes straight through light in vacuum, so for light to affect light it must go through matter. Yet the electric field that holds an electron to its atom is about 5×10¹¹ V/m. Squeeze 1 W into a 1 µm² cross-section of a silicon waveguide and the light’s field inside is about 1.5×10⁷ V/m, roughly one thirty-thousandth of that (field from I=12cε0nE2). With the light’s pull on the electron cloud this small compared with the atom’s hold on the electron, the spring barely stretches, and the Kerr effect is smaller by the square of this ratio. This is also why heat, carriers and saturable absorption are far larger than the Kerr effect. Heat and charges keep building up while the light shines, so even with a weak instantaneous field they can collect a large change over time. In effect, they buy size with time.

Reading the table down the columns shows the trade promised in section 1. Going from the Kerr effect to carriers to heat, as the response gets slower, the index change from the same light gets larger, because the longer a change is stored in the material, the larger the change a weak instantaneous field can collect. And the process that builds up that change is almost always absorption, so the routes that gain size also take on loss and heat. Only the Kerr effect works instantly without absorption, but it is too small. Make up for that smallness with a cavity, and now the cavity linewidth and photon lifetime tie down speed and bandwidth. The optoelectronic hybrid escapes this trade by getting its nonlinearity from circuit gain instead of from the material, and pays by bringing electricity back in.

Choosing where to get the nonlinearity of an optical neural network is, in the end, choosing one row of this table. If a layer should finish within nanoseconds, heat is out; if loss cannot be tolerated, saturable absorption and carriers are a burden; if it must work at low intensity, the Kerr effect is out. Because no row fills every column, optical nonlinearity remains an open problem.
