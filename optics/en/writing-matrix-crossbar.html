How can a neural network be expressed as a crossbar?

← All essays

This essay started while reading papers from Professor Wolfram Pernice’s group at the Kirchhoff Institute for Physics (KIP), Heidelberg University. Optical neural network papers often represent a neural network as a crossbar model, and I never understood that part, which kept me from understanding the papers as a whole. So this time I will work through, in the order I got stuck, why a grid-shaped photonic circuit becomes a neural network.

2026-10-28 · Structures › Microring weight bank · PCM crossbar

The starting point — two circuits made of grids

Reading many papers, I found that even with different devices, they often used circuits with the same structure. For example, the Pernice group’s phase-change material (PCM) photonic tensor core uses a PCM patch at each crossing, and the Princeton group’s microring weight bank uses ring resonators, as weight devices. The Princeton group’s abstract even contained this sentence.

From the abstract (paraphrased)We report the first observations of a recurrent silicon photonic neural network, in which connections are configured by microring weight banks. Using a bifurcation analysis of the dynamics, we show that this silicon photonic circuit is mathematically isomorphic to a continuous neural network model.

Why can a circuit of devices laid out in a grid be called “the same thing” as a neural network? The neural networks I had seen were drawings of nodes in one layer connected to nodes in the next, while the photonic circuit just looked like a grid. Most other optical computing papers have this grid structure too, so let me think it through once and write it down.

The structure of a neural network

Let us start with the structure of a neural network. A neural network is usually drawn with nodes (circles) and lines. It looks like a picture that simply shows connectivity between nodes, but nodes and lines each have their own job.

  • Line: connects xⱼ and yᵢ. When passing the value of xⱼ to yᵢ, it multiplies by the weight wᵢⱼ.
  • Node: adds up all the values coming from other nodes, adds a bias, and sends out a single value through the activation function σ.

So node x₂ sends the same value to y₁, y₂ and y₃, but as it passes along each line it is multiplied differently, by w₁₂, w₂₂ and w₃₂. In effect y₁, y₂ and y₃ each hear x₂’s signal at a different volume.

If x₂’s three lines had no weights, or all the same weight, y₁, y₂ and y₃ would receive the same value from x₂. If the same held for x₁ and x₃, the three neurons would end up computing the same value, and having several neurons would be pointless. Putting one weight on each receiving node is not the answer either: the neuron would then only add up its inputs and multiply by one number, unable to make distinctions like “listen closely to x₁ and ignore x₃”. So the weights have to be separate for every line — 9 of them for 3×3. The nodes and lines in the figure do not just show connections; each carries its own computation.

Turning it into a matrix

The three equations in figure 2 repeat the same shape. Organize them as a table with wᵢⱼ in “row i for the receiver, column j for the sender”, and it becomes a matrix as is.

Expanding the matrix product Wx row by row gives yᵢ = wᵢ₁x₁ + wᵢ₂x₂ + wᵢ₃x₃. This row is exactly “neuron i receives values from every neuron in the previous layer and takes a weighted sum”, written as an equation. That is why a fully connected layer in a neural-network library is computed internally as a single matrix multiplication. The graph is a picture drawn for people; the computation is always done as a matrix.

The bias b and activation function σ left in the equation will be handled separately after looking at the crossbar.

The crossbar — why a grid becomes matrix multiplication

Now back to the grid of figure 1. Lay the matrix’s columns as vertical lines and its rows as horizontal lines, put one weight device wherever two lines meet, and you get that grid. This is the crossbar. Writing w and the light paths into the figure that first stumped me gives this.

The three parts of the crossbar each work like this.

  • Input xⱼ: carries the value on the intensity of laser light. A modulator sets the intensity; brighter light is a larger value.
  • Crossing box w: sends a fraction wᵢⱼ of the light coming down onto the horizontal line. This is the multiplication.
  • Output Σ: the photodetector at the end of the horizontal line adds up all the incoming light intensity into one current. The addition needs no separate computation; the detector does it by itself.

So one vertical line is the three lines that node xⱼ sends out, and one horizontal line is the three lines coming into yᵢ.

The “mathematical isomorphism” in the abstract refers to this correspondence. It does not mean the pictures look alike; it means that writing down, physically as it is, how light splits, shrinks and gathers in the circuit gives the same equation as y = Wx. Each cell of the matrix pairs with one device in the circuit, and the order of multiplying and adding is the same. That is why weights trained in software can be transferred directly into their paired devices.

The crossing is a black box

So far the crossing device has been treated only as “a box that sends part of the light it receives toward the output”. That is the only property a crossbar needs to become matrix multiplication, so what goes inside the box is the designer’s choice. This is also where the two circuits of figure 1 part ways.

  • Microring resonator — a ring-shaped waveguide that resonates only light of a specific wavelength and passes it to the neighboring waveguide. Changing the ring’s temperature with a heater moves the resonance wavelength, changing how much light is passed over. Holding a weight means powering the heater continuously, and it is sensitive to ambient temperature changes. Each input uses a different wavelength, so the vertical lines are distinguished by wavelength.
  • Phase-change material (PCM) — a material whose light absorption changes as it moves between crystalline and amorphous states. Change the state once with a pulse and the value holds without power. But rewriting is slow and the number of rewrites is limited, so it suits inference with fixed weights. The vertical lines are distinguished by position on the chip.

Either way, the skeleton of the crossbar is the same; changing the device only changes power, speed, and whether it can be used for training.

Bias and activation function

The crossbar takes you as far as y = Wx. The equation of one neural-network layer, y = σ(Wx + b), still has b and σ left.

The bias can be folded into the matrix. Add one more input channel that is always 1, and the weights on that channel’s vertical line are b. In a photonic circuit this is handled by adding one more light channel that is always on at a constant intensity, or by adding a constant offset voltage on the electrical side.

The activation function cannot be left out. Without σ, stacking layers collapses to W₂(W₁x) = (W₂W₁)x — a single matrix. A hundred linear layers can do only what one linear layer does. But light is bad at this part. Photons barely interact with each other, so linear operations that split, attenuate and gather light are easy, while nonlinearities like “bend once past a threshold” are hard to make with light alone. So most optical neural networks turn the output light into current with photodetectors, apply σ in electronic circuits, and send it back out as light. The Princeton paper drove a modulator directly with the photodetector current, so that the modulator’s voltage–transmission curve plays the role of σ.

A question — earlier crossings affect later ones

But stare at figure 4 long enough and something odd shows up. As x₁’s light goes down the vertical line, part of it turns off onto a horizontal line at every crossing. So the lower the crossing, the less light is left. If all three crossings are set to take half, y₁, y₂ and y₃ receive 0.5, 0.25 and 0.125. The neural-network structure said “a node sends the same value down every line, and each line multiplies by its weight before passing it to the output”, but in the photonic circuit that is not what happens.

Light that has passed an earlier crossing is already reduced by what that crossing took. So the weight y₂ actually receives from x₁ is not the w₂₁ set in the device, but that value multiplied by the fraction left after the crossing above. It is this.

W₂₁(effective) = (1 − w₁₁) · w₂₁

There are two ways to deal with this.

1. Split evenly first with a splitter

The first is to give every row a fair share of the input. Instead of passing light down the vertical line in turn, a fixed splitter first divides the input light equally, 1/N to every row. The crossing device only attenuates the light it receives. Then each cell always receives 1/N however much the other cells use, and the value set in the device is the matrix value.

The price is signal strength. Each cell receives 1/N from the start, so the most it can send out is also 1/N, and the more rows there are, the weaker the output and the more easily it is buried in noise. The structure of the Tait paper that started this essay (broadcast-and-weight) works this way.

2. Set values that account for the attenuation

The second is to keep the serial structure and set device values that account for the reduction. If the desired W is fixed, compute backward from the top. For example, to give 0.7 to y₁ and 0.2 to y₂: the first device takes 0.7, and the second is set to 0.2 ÷ 0.3 ≈ 0.67 so that it takes 0.2 of the remaining 0.3. To split equally among three, as on the right of figure 5, take 1/3, 1/2 and all from the top down.

Finding them by training is even simpler. Write the circuit’s actual behavior as an equation and backpropagate with respect to the device values, and the influence of earlier crossings on later ones is included in the computation, finding attenuation-aware values by itself.

But the attenuation itself does not go away. Lower cells receive less light, so their signal is weaker, and since changing the device value changes the output less, training is slower too.

So which is better? Tuned as on the right of figure 5, even the serial structure lets every cell receive 1/N, so it can do everything the splitter does and can also share light among cells. The splitter, in turn, ties each cell to 1/N but keeps cells from affecting each other. Which of this flexibility and independence pays off in real training and noise, I have not yet found an answer to. Following which structure real chip papers chose, and why, is the question for the next essay.

Summary and limits

A neural network’s full connection is a matrix to begin with, and the weights are on the lines, not the nodes. A crossbar lays that matrix out directly on a photonic circuit, so one crossing device is one line. Whether a ring or PCM goes at the crossing is the next choice. The isomorphism in the paper meant that this pairing holds exactly in the equations, not as a metaphor.

But the grid handles only linear operations. The nonlinear activation function is handled separately by photodetectors and electronic circuits, or by modulators. Several rows share the light of one vertical line, so the larger the crossbar, the weaker the output, and passive devices cannot amplify light, so the signal must be boosted again when converting to electricity at each layer. Whether to split that light with a splitter or tap it in series is the question left above. And though not covered in this essay, light intensity cannot be negative, so how to make negative weights is a separate problem to solve.
