What Is a Qubit?
— Drawing It on the Bloch Sphere

Reader EE / programming background Scope Waves → Qubit → Bloch sphere → Measurement Prerequisite None

My background is optical computing. Then a chance to enter a quantum-algorithm competition came up, so lately I've been learning quantum computing. I'd worked with machine learning (LLMs) before and figured quantum would be similar — but the moment I started writing competition circuits, it was quite different. Coding while ignoring what a qubit actually is, nothing would work. So this piece is my own attempt to pin down "the minimum intuition needed to write quantum algorithms." The goal is to reach one single picture — drawing a qubit on the Bloch sphere.

Math is kept to a minimum; this runs on analogies and pictures. It's not a rigorous textbook but a notebook to get my imagination going — so in places I've chosen intuition over precision, and there may be leaps.

01

Waves and Wave-Particle Duality

The "quantum" in a quantum computer — the qubit — is, in one line, something in which 0 and 1 are superposed until you observe it. This doesn't mean it "stores" both at once; it means that before you measure, the value isn't pinned to a single outcome. And having several values overlap isn't such a foreign property — just as a wave carries many crests and troughs superposed together, very small particles like electrons also behave like waves (wave-particle duality). So to understand the qubit, the quickest path is to start from waves.

The most famous evidence is the double-slit experiment. Fire electrons at two narrow slits and an interference pattern appears on the screen — the kind of banding only a wave produces. The astonishing part is that the pattern builds up even when you fire the electrons one at a time. It means a single electron behaves as if it passed through both slits and interferes with itself — you can't say "it went through one particular slit."

But the moment you observe "which slit it went through," the interference pattern vanishes. Observation here doesn't mean a person "looking" — for instance, if you fire a photon at the electron to pin down its position, that interaction destroys the electron's wave nature, and it's fixed as having passed through a single slit, like a particle. The instant the path is revealed, the wave nature disappears — this one fact runs through all of quantum mechanics.

02

Bit vs. Qubit

A classical (electrical) bit is a switch. On or off, 1 or 0, nothing in between.

A qubit, though, has |0⟩ and |1⟩ overlapping until you observe it — the very property the double-slit electron showed when it acted as if it went through both slits (superposition). (The bar-and-bracket around |0⟩ is quantum notation for a "state," called a ket.) The coefficient in front of a state is its amplitude, and the square of that amplitude's magnitude is the probability of getting that state. So the superposition of 0 and 1, written out, is:

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ α, β: the complex amplitude of each component

With this notation, when you measure you get |0⟩ with probability |α|² and |1⟩ with probability |β|². One of the two must come out, so |α|² + |β|² = 1.

A qubit is not an abstract idea — it is a real physical thing. It can be built from many physical systems: superconducting circuits, trapped ions, neutral atoms, photons, and more. Whatever it is made of, the common thread is that it is a system with two states (a two-level system) to serve as 0 and 1 — or one you deliberately pick two levels out of.

From the EE side

A digital node is a single scalar, High or Low. What makes a qubit different is that its value is a combination of two amplitudes α, β, and because those amplitudes are complex numbers they carry not just a "magnitude" but also a phase. That phase turns out to be decisive later on.

03

The Bloch Sphere — Making a Qubit Visible

Now a problem shows up. We wrote the state out in full as α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ — but you can't see it. Since α, β are complex numbers, that is four real numbers' worth of information, and four dimensions can't be drawn.

Fortunately, two constraints cut down the degrees of freedom:

  • The probabilities sum to 1 (|α|²+|β|²=1) — once one is fixed, the other follows.
  • A phase applied to the whole state is invisible — multiplying both α, β by the same overall phase changes nothing, neither in measurement nor under a gate (just as rotating two photos together leaves their relationship unchanged). So that "global phase" isn't counted; the only thing that matters is the relative phase between α and β.

Putting it together — four real numbers minus two constraints — leaves exactly two degrees of freedom. Those two are θ (the polar angle), which acts like latitude, and φ, which acts like longitude. Using them, just as you pin a spot on a globe by latitude and longitude, we can draw the once-invisible qubit as a picture — and that picture is the Bloch sphere. (Earlier we said "the global phase is ignored, only the relative phase matters" — that relative phase is precisely this φ.) Rewriting the state in these two angles:

|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiφ sin(θ/2)|1⟩ θ: how far it tilts from the North pole (probability) · φ: longitude (phase)

The North pole is |0⟩, the South pole is |1⟩, and the qubit is an arrow on that surface.

θ is how far the arrow tilts away from the North pole. By the rule above, cos(θ/2) is the amplitude of 0, so on measurement you get 0 with probability cos²(θ/2) — in other words, θ sets the measurement probability. At the North pole (θ=0°) it's 100% 0, at the equator (θ=90°) it's 50:50, and at the South pole (θ=180°) it's 100% 1. φ is which direction (longitude) the arrow faces around the equator — this is the phase.

But why does this phase matter? Spin the arrow all the way around the equator — change only the phase — and the 0/1 probabilities don't move (they're set by θ alone). So it's natural to ask, "why does it matter?" The answer: it's invisible right now, but when a gate rotates the arrow, this phase completely changes the outcome. It's the hidden second knob. (Two states with the same probabilities but different phases respond differently to a gate — the concrete example comes in Part 2, on gates.)

It's faster to just play with it than to read about it. Drag the sphere below to rotate it, and move the arrow with the θ and φ sliders — in particular, watch how changing θ moves the probability bars while spinning φ (longitude) alone doesn't budge them one bit.

04

Measurement — The Moment the Wave Collapses

Recall the double slit from ①. The instant you observe it, the electron's wave nature collapses and settles onto a single path. A qubit is the same. However tilted the arrow is — at the equator or anywhere else — the moment you measure, the result is always either 0 or 1. There is no answer like "45 degrees."

Which way it lands is a matter of probability — as we saw, |0⟩ with probability |α|² and |1⟩ with probability |β|². The more the arrow tilts toward the North pole, the more often 0 comes up, but tilting doesn't guarantee that value — even a state pointing toward 0 can come out as 1 (it is, after all, only a probability). When you measure, the arrow jumps to the pole of whatever result just came out (collapse), and the superposition and phase information from before the measurement is gone — irreversibly.

There's a trap here. A single measurement can't tell you the probability. Even for a state with a 70% chance of 0, one measurement won't tell you whether it will be 0 or 1. So in practice you run the same circuit hundreds to thousands of times (shots) and recover the probabilities from the ratio of 0s to 1s. (The competition circuits I ran also measured 1024 shots each time.) A single result can't be trusted, so you measure many times, pull the answer out statistically, and filter out errors — that is what running quantum algorithms is really about.

From the EE side

It's similar to how an ADC quantizes a continuous voltage into a discrete code. But two things differ — which code it lands on is probabilistic, and the sampling destroys the original. An ADC reads the same waveform if you measure again, but for a qubit the first measurement changes the state itself.

That's why half of designing a quantum algorithm isn't "how do I compute" but "how do I stack the probabilities beforehand so that, when measured, the value corresponding to the correct answer comes up more often" — and figuring out which direction the correct answer lies in is the other half. The dice are cast the moment you measure, so rotating the arrow ahead of time with gates to make those dice fall toward the answer — that's the next story.
